## The judging framework: four dimensions

This is the highest-priority section of the entire dossier. Distinguish official statements from strategic elaboration.

> **Official evaluation dimensions**: 1. Opportunity for impact  |  2. Degree of difficulty  |  3. Novelty  |  4. Feasibility. Tony Zanders identified these at approximately 07:50-11:50 in the October 2 session. No points or percentages were assigned publicly. [T]

### Criterion 1. Opportunity for impact

Official meaning: Does the solution meet a need beyond the maker, neighborhood or one-off use? Could it help large groups, even internationally? The presentation does not require a proven million-user market, but it needs a credible impact opportunity. [T 07:55-08:45]

- Evidence: clear target user, interview or survey insights, published prevalence/scale data, current workaround, quantified time/cost/safety problem, plausible adoption pathway.

- Weak: "everyone needs this" or "AI will revolutionize the world" without proof.

- Strong: "X user group currently faces Y bottleneck; here is our tested workflow that reduces the bottleneck on these examples."

### Criterion 2. Degree of difficulty

Official meaning: Is there genuinely hard engineering or research? If an audience member can reproduce the project in two weeks with readily available tools, it is less defensible. Organizers said technically intensive hardware and deep tech may be scored more favorably than easily replicated software. [T 08:45-09:45, 38:15-40:15]

- Evidence: novel algorithm/architecture, specialized sensor/calibration pipeline, robust data engineering, domain constraints, experiments, advanced integration, failure analyses or real-time performance improvements.

- Weak: ordinary API integration with standard templates presented as a breakthrough.

- Strong: diagram of the hard subsystem, baseline comparison, reproducible test, and explanation of why the chosen design is necessary.

### Criterion 3. Novelty

Official meaning: Is the solution meaningfully different from what already exists? Tony asked whether a judge could quickly find many similar websites through an AI search. You are not required to show a conventional business competitor slide, but you should have done that thinking. [T 09:45-10:40]

- Evidence: direct competitors, research papers, open-source libraries, patents if relevant, current limitations, and a clearly demonstrated technical delta.

- Weak: "first ever" assertions or "nobody does this" without searching.

- Strong: state three closest alternatives and specify what measurable problem each fails to solve.

### Criterion 4. Feasibility

Official meaning: Does this technology have a credible path forward, and can the awarded capital help advance the current prototype? This is not the same as a mature revenue forecast. [T 10:40-11:50]

- Evidence: functional demonstration, reproducible setup, constraints, next experiment, parts/cloud costs, realistic milestones and what the prize would buy.

- Weak: dependency on unavailable datasets or unproven science hidden behind a polished mockup.

- Strong: demo works today, limitations are transparent, and the next $10,000 has identifiable technical uses.

Evidence: [T 07:50-11:50; 38:15-40:15]. All "evidence/weak/strong" examples above are advisory synthesis.

## Judge simulation and evidence standards

An agent should challenge its own ideas rather than flatter them.

### Eight uncomfortable judge questions

**1. What did you actually build?.** State the implemented core. Separate built parts, third-party services, simulated outputs and future features.

**2. What is new?.** Name at least three comparable solutions or papers and the specific technical delta.

**3. Why is this difficult?.** Explain technical constraints, engineering effort and why a simple baseline cannot reach the same result.

**4. How do you know it works?.** Show a small reproducible evaluation with realistic samples and failure cases.

**5. Who needs it?.** Identify a user group and evidence of a painful workflow or unmet need.

**6. Can it scale?.** Describe architectural and operational limits without inventing traction.

**7. What will prize money change?.** Budget the next prototype milestone, not an imaginary global rollout.

**8. Can you repeat this demo?.** Document setup, dependencies, safe example data, fallback video and recovery path.

> **Scoring is unofficial**: Use a private 0-to-5 score for each official criterion and optional "demo readiness" or "team fit" gates, but label the scoring as a decision aid. It is not the Nexus scorecard. Reject ideas that cannot achieve a real demonstration before the application cutoff.

### Recommended evidence packet

- A working artifact: build, prototype link, recording or functional hardware.

- An architecture diagram: inputs, processing, novel contribution, outputs, dependencies.

- A comparison table: baseline, competitors, tested difference, source for every claim.

- An evaluation record: test set, metric definition, actual results, limitations and failure examples.

- An implementation log: what each teammate built, timestamps or commits, help from mentors.

### The competitor comparison test

For each proposed idea, research five buckets before claiming novelty: (1) direct consumer/commercial products, (2) open-source solutions, (3) peer-reviewed research, (4) government/university prototypes, and (5) prior Tech Cup or Nexus DevDays entries. Save URLs, publish dates, screenshots, constraints and a direct comparison. An idea is not novel because the team has never heard of it.

### What a defensible technical advantage looks like

- A verified capability competitors lack for your chosen population or environment.

- A measurable improvement in error rate, latency, privacy, cost, robustness or interpretability.

- A new algorithm, sensor integration, data-validation technique or hard deployment constraint.

- An original integration that solves a previously unaddressed engineering bottleneck, not merely combining APIs.

## Brainstorming: how an AI agent should search

Generate grounded project hypotheses, not grandiose startup slogans.

### Start from pain, not from technology

**1. Find a user with a painful workflow.** Look at public agencies, teachers, farmers, clinics, families, industrial operators, accessibility groups, small businesses or university labs. Do not pretend interviews happened.

**2. Measure the problem.** Identify an observable cost, delay, error, safety risk or unmet need. Use citations for statistics.

**3. Identify the technical bottleneck.** Find the reason existing solutions fail: missing data, poor robustness, hard environments, domain rules, latency, auditability, price or poor UX.

**4. Check what already exists.** Collect close products, demos, papers, patents and available code before naming novelty.

**5. Define a 7-day proof.** Specify an end-to-end working demonstration using available tools and lawful, permissioned data.

**6. Invent a test.** Define a small benchmark or repeatable evaluation that can expose both wins and failures.

**7. Kill weak ideas early.** Reject concept-only ideas, unsupported clinical/safety claims, unavailable hardware, secret data and indistinguishable API wrappers.

### Good areas for exploring high-impact problems

The Nexus site broadly features healthcare access, environment/coastal resilience and education, while the session emphasized all industries are welcome. Additional plausible problem spaces include agriculture, infrastructure, disaster resilience, energy, accessibility, local logistics, software assurance and university research workflows. These are idea search spaces, not official award preferences. [W1, W6, T]

User: Agricultural advisers. Potential technical delta: Link plot, crop, weather constraints and pesticide label versions to auditable requirements. Demo: controlled sample decision with citations and conflicting rules. Risk: FarmMind is already a relevant competitor; misuse harms crops.

### 12  Assistive maintenance fault explainer

User: School facilities / small industrial firms. Potential technical delta: Use low-cost vibration/audio samples and calibrated uncertainty to distinguish common equipment faults. Demo: seeded equipment conditions or labeled public dataset. Risk: noisy sensing and real deployment accuracy.

## Six practical risk controls

Avoid disqualifying mistakes and protect your teammates and users.

**1. Intellectual property.** A patent is not mandatory or automatically rewarded. But public demonstrations can jeopardize future patentability. Consult university technology transfer if disclosure matters. [T 33:45-36:45]

**2. Accuracy and domain safety.** For health, farming, emergency, legal or infrastructure projects, never claim professional reliability without tests. Label prototypes and require qualified human oversight.

**3. AI contributions and originality.** Track team-created code and design. Disclose important third-party data, open source and hosted models where relevant; the form includes an originality affirmation. [W2]

**4. Team awards.** Every eligible teammate must be named on the submitted application to share prize money under the organizers' described policy. [T 46:45-47:30]

**5. Data rights and privacy.** Use synthetic or permissioned datasets for video and public demos. Never leak private university, patient, client or company data.

**6. Honest metrics.** A one-sample success is not a 99% accuracy claim. Record sample sizes, baseline definition and failure cases, and do not overstate results.

## Copy-ready agent prompt: implement and evaluate

Hand this template to a coding agent only after the team selects an idea and approves its constraints.

### BUILD CONTRACT

Create the smallest honest end-to-end implementation proving the candidate's unique technical idea. Prefer one highly credible flow to a dozen incomplete features. Clearly document external models, APIs, data and open source dependencies.

### ACCEPTANCE TESTS

Specify test inputs, expected outputs and pass conditions before coding. Test normal, adverse and out-of-distribution cases, plus integration failure. Log actual results and avoid claiming external validation.

### NOVELTY EVIDENCE

Implement a baseline representing the simplest existing approach. Compare on a justified metric such as factual support, latency, false alerts, cost, memory, recall or task completion.

### DEMO REQUIREMENTS

Produce a reproducible 60- to 90-second flow showing problem, input, key processing, output and differentiator. Provide a longer backup demo recording and readable screenshots.

### ARTIFACTS

### STOP CONDITIONS

Stop or escalate if data privacy is violated, false claims are required, legal/clinical/safety claims exceed evidence, IP disclosures are at risk, or hardware access is impossible.

### COMPLETION

Report what works, what does not, what was simulated, test metrics with sample size, remaining risks, and the exact content that can honestly be placed into the Nexus application.
